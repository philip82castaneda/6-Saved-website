Common Situations

If ownership has started to feel heavy, you're not alone

A lot of owners we talk to are simply worn out — not from one bad year, but from the accumulation of small things.

The repair list never ends

Roofs, HVAC, plumbing, and code items don't wait for a convenient time. Multi-unit buildings mean multiple systems, multiplied — and deferred maintenance only gets more expensive the longer it sits.

Tenants keep turning over

Screening applicants, chasing late rent, handling move-outs, and turning units between tenants is a full-time responsibility most owners didn't sign up for long-term.

It's just time to move on

Whether you're retiring, consolidating your holdings, or inherited the building and never wanted to run it, it's completely reasonable to decide this isn't the chapter you want anymore.

What Repairs Really Cost

Renovation isn't cheap at this scale

For context, here's what bringing a small multifamily building up to a competitive standard typically runs — before financing, permits, or the income lost while units sit vacant during the work.

Building size Light cosmetic refresh Full renovation Gut renovation
4 units $32,000 – $60,000 $100,000 – $180,000 $200,000 – $360,000
6 units $48,000 – $90,000 $150,000 – $270,000 $300,000 – $540,000
12 units $96,000 – $180,000 $300,000 – $540,000 $600,000 – $1,080,000

Estimates based on typical per-unit ranges of roughly $8,000–$15,000 (paint, flooring, fixtures), $25,000–$45,000 (kitchens, baths, mechanical updates), and $50,000–$90,000+ (layout changes and major system replacement) for small multifamily properties. Actual costs vary by market and scope, and building-wide items like roofing or a shared HVAC system are often extra, regardless of unit count.

Numbers like these are usually the real reason an owner decides it's time to sell rather than reinvest — especially when the building still has to generate rent, or sit vacant, while the work gets done.
The Old Way

What a traditional sale asks of a landlord

Listing an occupied or repair-heavy multi-unit building usually means:

— Making repairs before you can list, or accepting a lower offer because you didn't
— Coordinating showings around tenants' schedules — or dealing with tenants who won't cooperate
— Investor buyers whose financing depends on rent rolls, inspections, and appraisals that can fall through late
— Sitting on a property that still needs insurance, taxes, and upkeep every month it's on the market
— Continuing to field maintenance calls and vacancies the entire time it's listed
